# Hi, I'm Josh Powell.

I work where data, operations, and technology meet—the part nobody notices when it's working and everybody notices when it isn't.

I've spent 15+ years in IT without staying in one neat lane. I've run my own IT consulting business, supported networks and desktops, worked with pharmacy systems, handled healthcare and pharmacy EDI, and managed enterprise master data across interconnected systems.

That variety taught me one thing: the flashy technology is rarely the whole problem. The real trouble usually starts underneath—in data nobody trusts, ownership nobody defined, or a process that only works because one person knows all of its exceptions.

That's where I work best.

## The longer version

When something breaks, I want to fix the immediate problem—but I also keep asking why. What caused it? Why did the process allow it? What would prevent it from happening again?

The technology is rarely the problem by itself. Sometimes a process creates shadow data in offline spreadsheets because the official system is too difficult to use. Sometimes the right tool exists, but nobody was trained to use it properly. Sometimes a better tool is sitting there unused because no one knew it was available. Fixing the visible error without fixing the process underneath it just schedules the next support call.

My career started close to the equipment and the people using it. Support could mean replacing the motherboard in an executive's laptop or maintaining ISP towers in the middle of the night. I've done both.

Systems support taught me that a technically correct answer is useless if it doesn't solve the actual problem. Consulting taught me to explain what was broken without hiding behind jargon. Healthcare technology added a harder requirement: changes had to work without getting in the way of patient care.

From there, I moved deeper into corporate systems and data. I supported large pharmacy go-lives, worked on data-center migrations, established EDI pipelines, and spent years troubleshooting the places where systems, vendors, and real-world operations stopped agreeing with one another.

My master-data work has covered worldwide sales, vendor, employee, shipping, customer, and material records—including more than 1,200 parts—along with integrations across SAP instances and the SharePoint pages supporting those processes.

Master data gets dismissed as cleanup work. It isn't. It's the foundation underneath purchasing, manufacturing, reporting, automation, and every AI system expected to make a useful decision.

Bad data doesn't become smart because you put a better model on top of it. It just fails faster and with more confidence.

## One project that pulls this together

My [Mac Pro OpenClaw infrastructure case study](https://github.com/smilidon/macpro-openclaw-infrastructure-case-study) documents how I repurposed a 2013 Mac Pro instead of discarding it. The machine now provides a durable home for Thumbo, the career-support OpenClaw agent I built to help with résumé materials, research, organization, and interview and job-search preparation—with me reviewing and approving consequential work.

The point was never to force every AI workload onto old GPUs. It was to give capable hardware a useful job, route work honestly, and document what it can and cannot do.

The project now includes one bounded short-task local-model qualification, still manual, human-reviewed, and not deployed as an OpenClaw route.

## How I work

- Find the root cause, not just the error message.
- Test the assumption before building around it.
- Keep ownership clear and documentation plain.
- Understand the process before automating it.
- Say what failed, not just what made the demo.
- Treat reliable data as infrastructure, not an afterthought.
